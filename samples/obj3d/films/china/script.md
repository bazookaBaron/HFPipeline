Facts and sources (approx. = estimate or rounded; allegation = attributed claim; checked 2026-11-14):
- Shanzhai (山寨) = "mountain stronghold", bandit hideout beyond official control; later the word for knock-off electronics (Wikipedia "Shanzhai"; Chubb, Univ. of Michigan 2015).
- Shenzhen SEZ created Aug 1980 (border county of farms/fishing villages); 2020 census 17,560,061 people (Shenzhen Statistics Bureau).
- Huaqiangbei: ~1.45 km2, ~35 electronics markets, ~115,000 businesses (Futian district / Huaqiangbei subdistrict figures) (approx.).
- MediaTek (Taiwan) turnkey phone platform (chip + software reference design) from ~2006 slashed phone design effort (Stanford GSB case "MediaTek and the shanzhai"; The Economist 2009).
- Shanzhai phone output: ~145m (2009), ~228m (2010) iSuppli estimates (approx.) -> "well over a hundred million a year at the peak". Dual-SIM, loud speakers, torches, TV tuners common features (Wired 2009; Stanford case).
- Car JVs: 1994 Automotive Industry Policy capped foreign ownership at 50%; lifted for NEV makers 2018, commercial vehicles 2020, passenger cars 1 Jan 2022 (NDRC; Reuters 2018/2022). VW-SAIC JV founded 1984, GM-SAIC 1997.
- WTO accession 11 Dec 2001; Accession Protocol: no technology-transfer conditions on investment approval (WTO WT/L/432).
- USTR Section 301 report, 22 Mar 2018: found China uses JV requirements, equity limits and administrative approvals to pressure technology transfer (USTR). China's MOFCOM rejected the findings (Reuters 2018).
- Foreign Investment Law adopted 15 Mar 2019, in force 1 Jan 2020, Art. 22 bans forced technology transfer by administrative means (NPC text).
- AMSC/Sinovel: Sinovel stopped paying 2011 (~$800m in contracts unpaid); AMSC employee Dejan Karabasevic in Austria paid to pass source code, convicted in Austria 2011; Sinovel convicted by US jury Jan 2018 (W.D. Wis.); DOJ: AMSC lost >$1bn in shareholder equity and almost 700 jobs; max $1.5m fine; $57.5m settlement with AMSC, July 2018 (DOJ press releases 2018; AMSC 8-K).
- Micron/Fujian Jinhua: DOJ indictment Nov 2018 (UMC + Jinhua, trade secrets via ex-Micron Taiwan engineers); UMC pleaded guilty Oct 2020, $60m fine; Jinhua acquitted 27 Feb 2024 in a bench trial (Judge Maxine Chesney, N.D. Cal.) (DOJ; Reuters 2024).
- May 2014: DOJ indicted five PLA Unit 61398 officers for hacking US companies (Westinghouse, US Steel, Alcoa, SolarWorld, ATI, USW); none tried (DOJ 2014).
- IP Commission 2017 update: theft of US IP (counterfeits, pirated software, trade secrets) costs $225-600bn a year (ESTIMATE, all sources, not China only); calls China the "principal infringer"; China + Hong Kong = 87% of seized counterfeits (IP Commission report 2017).
- FBI Director Wray, 7 Jul 2020 (Hudson Institute): FBI opens a new China-related counterintelligence case "about every 10 hours" (FBI).
- High-speed rail: 2004 tenders won by Kawasaki, Alstom, Bombardier, (2005) Siemens with technology-transfer conditions; CRH2 based on Kawasaki E2-1000 Shinkansen, CRH3 on Siemens Velaro; Kawasaki executives objected to Chinese patent filings/exports (WSJ 2011; FT 2011); CR400 "Fuxing" 2017 billed as indigenous (Xinhua); network ~48,000 km end 2024 (approx., China State Railway Group), passed 50,000 km Dec 2025; more than two-thirds of the world's HSR (UIC).
- DeepSeek R1 released 20 Jan 2025; V3 paper: final training run $5.576m in GPU time at an assumed $2/GPU-hour (excludes R&D, hardware) (DeepSeek-V3 tech report); SemiAnalysis estimated ~$1.6bn total server capex (different measure) (approx.).
- Nvidia 27 Jan 2025: -17%, ~$589bn (Bloomberg) / $593bn (Reuters) market value lost, largest one-day loss in US market history -> "about five hundred and ninety billion" (approx.).
- ALLEGATION: OpenAI told the FT (29 Jan 2025) it had seen some evidence of "distillation" by DeepSeek; David Sacks said there was "substantial evidence". OpenAI memo to the House China Select Committee, 12 Feb 2026. DeepSeek did not admit it.
- ALLEGATION: Anthropic, Feb 2026: >16 million exchanges via ~24,000 fraudulent accounts used to distil Claude, attributed to DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax. China's Foreign Ministry: "unfounded accusations and smears"; MOFCOM: "groundless".
- Benchmark claims: no documented case of a Chinese lab faking benchmarks; studies found signs of benchmark-data leakage (contamination), e.g. Qwen models (arXiv 2409.01790) -> scripted as "signs of leakage, not proof of cheating, and not only a Chinese problem".
- OpenAI itself sued over training data (NYT v. OpenAI, Dec 2023).
- BYD began making rechargeable (phone) batteries 1995; battery-electric cars: 2024 Tesla 1,789,226 vs BYD 1,764,992; 2025 BYD 2.257m vs Tesla 1.636m (company reports; Reuters Jan 2026).
- China >70% of global EV production 2024 (IEA Global EV Outlook 2025) -> "about seven in ten".
- CATL 37.9% of global EV battery installations 2024 (SNE Research) -> "more than a third".
- China >80% of every manufacturing stage of solar PV (IEA Solar PV Global Supply Chains, 2022).
- DJI roughly 70-80% of consumer drones (CONTESTED, industry estimates; DJI publishes none) -> "most of the world's consumer drones (approx.)".
- WIPO: China ~1.64m of 3.55m patent applications in 2023 (~46%) (WIPO IP Facts and Figures 2024) -> "nearly half".
- ASPI Critical Technology Tracker 2024: China leads research in 57 of 64 technologies (measures research output) (ASPI).
- Ford's Michigan battery plant (BlueOval Battery Park Marshall) uses CATL LFP technology under licence (Ford, Feb 2023).
- Britain banned emigration of skilled artisans 1719 (repealed 1824) and export of textile machinery from 1774 (lifted 1843) (Jeremy, "Damming the Flood", 1977).
- Samuel Slater left England 1789, rebuilt Arkwright's machines from memory, Pawtucket mill 1790, "Slater the Traitor" in Britain; Andrew Jackson called him "father of American manufactures" (Slater Mill NHP / NPS).
- Francis Cabot Lowell toured British mills 1810-12, memorised the power loom; Waltham mill 1814 (NPS Lowell).
- Alexander Hamilton, Report on Manufactures, 5 Dec 1791: proposed premiums/rewards for importing foreign improvements and inventions (Founders Online).
- US gave no copyright to foreign authors until the Chace Act 1891; Dickens attacked US piracy on his 1842 tour (Library of Congress).
- UK Merchandise Marks Act 1887 -> "Made in Germany" marking, meant to flag foreign goods passing as British; became a mark of quality (BBC 2012; Head, "Made in Germany", 1992).
- Ha-Joon Chang, "Kicking Away the Ladder" (Anthem, 2002): rich countries used protection and tech acquisition, then discouraged them (contested by critics).

#ch1 cold open
NARR (quiet, curious): In Shenzhen's Huaqiangbei market, a phone sits on a counter. The logo looks familiar.
NARR (dry): It has the shape, the box, even the startup sound. It costs a fraction of the real thing.
NARR (matter-of-fact): Around twenty ten, workshops here turned out phones like this by the hundred million a year.
NARR (dry): For decades, that was how the West pictured China. The copy machine.
NARR (explaining): Joint ventures that took the blueprints. Court cases over stolen code. Bullet trains built on foreign designs.
NARR (curious): And now artificial intelligence, where the same accusation is back.
NARR (building): But the same country now leads the world in electric cars, batteries, solar panels and drones.
NARR (curious): So how did the copy machine become the factory everyone wants to copy? And is copying how every country got rich?
#ch2 shanzhai
NARR (storytelling): In nineteen eighty, Shenzhen was a border county of farms and fishing villages, next to Hong Kong.
NARR (explaining): That year, Beijing made it a special economic zone. Cheap land, cheap labour, and foreign money welcome.
NARR (matter-of-fact): Factories came first. Toys, shoes, then electronics for foreign brands.
NARR (matter-of-fact): By the twenty twenty census, about seventeen and a half million people lived there.
NARR (explaining): In the middle of it sits Huaqiangbei. Around a hundred thousand businesses packed into one and a half square kilometres.
NARR (storytelling): Out of those streets came a word. Shanzhai. It means mountain stronghold, the hideout of bandits beyond the emperor's reach.
NARR (dry): By the two thousands it meant knock-offs. Nokia-ish phones. Samsung-shaped phones. Phones with very familiar logos.
NARR (explaining): The trick was a chip. A Taiwanese company, MediaTek, sold a turnkey kit: processor, software, almost a whole phone on one board.
NARR (explaining): A small workshop could design a phone around it, and buy every part a few streets away.
NARR (light): And they didn't only copy. Shanzhai phones had two SIM slots, huge speakers, built-in torches, even TV antennas.
NARR (dry): Features the big brands hadn't bothered with, for customers the big brands had ignored.
NARR (matter-of-fact): Analysts estimated over two hundred million shanzhai phones in twenty ten alone.
NARR (dry, pointed): Cheap, fast, and legally questionable. Hold that thought.
#ch3 the price of entry
NARR (explaining): Meanwhile, the big foreign companies wanted in. A billion customers, and the cheapest factories on Earth.
NARR (explaining): Beijing's price was a partner. From nineteen ninety-four, a foreign carmaker could own at most half of a Chinese car factory.
NARR (matter-of-fact): Volkswagen and General Motors both teamed up with Shanghai's state carmaker.
NARR (dry): The partner got the jobs, half the profits, and a front-row seat to how the cars were made.
NARR (explaining): In two thousand one, China joined the World Trade Organization, and promised not to make technology transfer a condition of entry.
NARR (dry): Foreign companies said the pressure just went quieter. Licences and approvals that moved faster for those who shared.
NARR (explaining): In twenty eighteen, a US trade investigation concluded that China used joint-venture rules and approvals to pressure firms into handing over technology.
NARR (explaining): Beijing rejected that. Officially, any transfer was a voluntary business deal.
NARR (matter-of-fact): Then the rules changed. The ownership cap was lifted for electric car makers in twenty eighteen, and for all passenger cars in twenty twenty-two.
NARR (matter-of-fact): And a new foreign investment law, in force from twenty twenty, banned forced technology transfer.
NARR (dry): By then, the partners had been taking notes for almost thirty years.
#ch4 the cases
NARR (serious): Some of what flowed out wasn't negotiated at all. Some of it, courts found, was stolen.
NARR (storytelling): Case one. American Superconductor sold the software that runs wind turbines to Sino-vel, a fast-growing Chinese turbine maker.
NARR (explaining): In twenty eleven, Sino-vel stopped paying. Then the American company found its own code running in Sino-vel's turbines.
NARR (explaining): One of its engineers in Austria had been paid to pass on the source code. An Austrian court convicted him.
NARR (matter-of-fact): In twenty eighteen, a US jury convicted Sino-vel of trade secret theft.
NARR (matter-of-fact): The US Justice Department said the American firm lost more than a billion dollars in shareholder value, and almost seven hundred jobs.
NARR (dry): Sino-vel settled for fifty seven and a half million dollars.
NARR (storytelling): Case two. Micron, the American memory chip maker. Prosecutors said engineers leaving its Taiwan unit took chip secrets to a partner of Fujian Jinhua, a state-backed chipmaker.
NARR (explaining): In twenty twenty, that partner, the Taiwanese firm UMC, pleaded guilty in the US and paid a sixty million dollar fine.
NARR (dry): Jin-hwah itself went to trial. In twenty twenty-four, a US judge acquitted it. Courts don't always agree with headlines.
NARR (explaining): Case three. In twenty fourteen, the US charged five Chinese military officers with hacking American companies. They have never stood trial.
NARR (matter-of-fact): So how big is all this? A US commission estimated that intellectual property theft costs America two hundred and twenty five to six hundred billion dollars a year.
NARR (explaining): It called China the principal infringer. But that range covers theft from every country, and it's a rough estimate.
NARR (dry): In twenty twenty, the FBI director said his agency opened a new China-related counterintelligence case about every ten hours.
#ch5 the bullet train
NARR (storytelling): Now the bullet train. In two thousand four, China asked the world's train makers to bid for high-speed trains.
NARR (explaining): Kawasaki of Japan, Alstom and Bombardier won, and Siemens a year later. The condition: build in China, with Chinese partners, and share the know-how.
NARR (explaining): China's first CRH2 trains were based on a Japanese Shinkansen. The CRH3 was based on Siemens' Velaro.
NARR (matter-of-fact): Within a few years, Chinese state firms were offering their own high-speed trains abroad, and filing patents.
NARR (dry): Kawasaki executives objected that their technology was being sold as Chinese. China said it had improved it enough to call it its own.
NARR (matter-of-fact): In twenty seventeen came the Fuxing, a train China calls fully its own design.
NARR (explaining): Today China has more high-speed rail than the rest of the world combined. Around forty eight thousand kilometres by twenty twenty-four.
NARR (dry): First they licensed it. Then absorbed, rebuilt, and scaled. Remember that pattern. It's about to matter.
#ch6 the AI fight
NARR (storytelling): January twenty twenty-five. A Chinese lab called DeepSeek released R1, a reasoning model that rivalled the best American ones.
NARR (explaining): It said the final training run of its base model cost about five point six million dollars in chip time. Analysts said the full bill was far higher.
NARR (matter-of-fact): On January the twenty seventh, Nvidia lost about five hundred and ninety billion dollars of market value in one day. A record.
NARR (serious): Then came the accusation. OpenAI said it had seen evidence that DeepSeek may have used distillation.
NARR (explaining): Distillation means training your model on another model's answers. Ask it millions of questions, keep the replies, learn from them.
NARR (explaining): In twenty twenty-six, Anthropic said it found more than sixteen million such exchanges, through about twenty four thousand fake accounts.
NARR (explaining): It blamed three Chinese labs: DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax. Beijing called the accusations groundless.
NARR (dry): Researchers also found signs that some Chinese models had seen benchmark test questions in training. That's a leak, not proof of cheating, and not only a Chinese problem.
NARR (dry, amused): And critics enjoyed one irony. OpenAI itself is being sued for training on other people's writing.
NARR (low): So is AI the copy machine again? The courts haven't ruled. But this time, the thing being copied is a mind.
#ch7 the turn
NARR (building): Now the turn. Look at what China makes today.
NARR (storytelling): BYD started out in nineteen ninety-five making rechargeable batteries for phones.
NARR (explaining): In twenty twenty-five, it sold more battery electric cars than Tesla. Two point two six million, against one point six four.
NARR (matter-of-fact): China builds about seven in ten of all electric cars made anywhere.
NARR (matter-of-fact): One Chinese company, CATL, makes more than a third of the world's electric car batteries.
NARR (explaining): For solar panels, China does more than eighty percent of every manufacturing step, from raw silicon to the finished panel.
NARR (matter-of-fact): One Shenzhen company, DJI, sells most of the world's consumer drones, by industry estimates.
NARR (explaining): And China now files nearly half of all the patent applications in the world.
NARR (explaining): An Australian think tank tracks sixty four critical technologies. In research output, China leads in fifty seven.
NARR (dry): Ford is building a battery plant in Michigan using CATL's technology, under licence.
NARR (dry, pointed): The joint-venture deal, in reverse.
#ch8 the ladder
NARR (curious): So was China cheating? Or doing what every rising power did?
NARR (storytelling): Rewind to seventeen eighty-nine. Britain guards its textile machines. Exporting them is illegal. So is skilled mill workers leaving.
NARR (storytelling): A young apprentice, Samuel Slater, memorises the machines in Richard Arkwright's mills and sails for America.
NARR (explaining): In Rhode Island he rebuilds them from memory. America calls him a father of its manufacturing. Britain calls him Slater the Traitor.
NARR (storytelling): Two decades later, a Boston merchant, Francis Cabot Low-ell, tours British mills, memorises the power loom, and builds his own at home.
NARR (explaining): Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton had proposed rewards for anyone who brought foreign inventions to America.
NARR (dry): And until eighteen ninety-one, America gave foreign authors no copyright at all. Charles Dickens toured the country complaining about pirated books.
NARR (dry): In eighteen eighty-seven, Britain made foreign goods say where they were from. Made in Germany started as a warning label, and became a badge of quality.
NARR (explaining): The economist Ha-Joon Chang calls this kicking away the ladder. Rich countries climbed by copying, then told everyone else not to.
NARR (serious): None of that makes hacking or stolen code legal. Theft is theft, and courts have punished it.
NARR (dry): But the ladder is real. And every industrial power has climbed it.
#ch9 ending
NARR (quiet): Back in Huaqiangbei, the counters still sell knock-offs. Right next to stalls selling China's own brands to the world.
NARR (quiet, dry): The copy machine learned to make originals. Now the rest of the world is taking notes.
NARR (dry, final): The question isn't whether China can copy anymore. It's whether anyone can keep up.
